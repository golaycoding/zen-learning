# Dependent and Independent Clauses

### Grade 7 Grammar Study Guide

A clause is a group of phrases with a subject and a verb. Clauses come in two types: independent, which can stand alone as a sentence, and dependent, which cannot. Knowing the difference — and being able to explain why a clause is one or the other — builds stronger, more skillful sentences.

## Resource: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sAo6LbCUAQo

## 1. Independent Clauses

An independent clause is basically a sentence. It is a collection of phrases with a subject and a verb (or a subject and a predicate) that expresses a complete thought.

- *I ate the pineapple.* — the noun phrase "I" is the subject, "ate the pineapple" is the verb phrase.
- *The bear roared.*
- *Maureen pointed out the monster.*
- *That's not our pet rabbit.*

## 2. Dependent Clauses

A dependent clause also has a subject and a verb, but it cannot stand on its own as a sentence. It often begins with a subordinating conjunction (because, although, while, unless) or a relative pronoun (that, which, who), and that word is what makes it dependent — it needs an independent clause to lean on.

- *Because it was delicious.*
- *While the salmon flopped.*
- *That she saw last night.*
- *Unless I'm mistaken.*

On its own, a dependent clause is a sentence fragment. Fragments are common in natural, informal speech ("Why did you eat the pizza?" "Because it was delicious.") but are avoided in formal writing.

## 3. Subordinating Conjunctions and Relative Pronouns

The word attached to the front of a dependent clause signals that it depends on other information:

- **Subordinating conjunctions:** because, although, while, unless, since
- **Relative pronouns:** that, which, who

Removing the subordinating conjunction can turn a dependent clause back into an independent one. *"It was delicious"* is a complete sentence; adding *"because"* to the front makes it dependent.

## 4. Combining Dependent and Independent Clauses

A dependent clause usually attaches to an independent clause to form a complete sentence. The independent clause carries the main idea; the dependent clause adds background information.

- *The bear roared while the salmon flopped.* — "The bear roared" is a complete sentence on its own; "while the salmon flopped" adds extra context.
- *Maureen pointed out the monster that she saw last night.* — the relative pronoun "that" refers back to "the monster," linking the dependent clause to it.
- *That's not our pet rabbit, unless I'm mistaken.* — "unless" signals the second clause depends on information in the first.

## 5. Spotting the Hidden Verb

Some dependent clauses hide their verb in a contraction. *"Unless I'm mistaken"* contains the verb "am" inside "I'm" (I am mistaken). Reading the clause carefully reveals the subject and verb even when compressed.

---

## Quick Reference Summary

- **Independent clause:** subject + verb; expresses a complete thought; can stand alone as a sentence
- **Dependent clause:** subject + verb + a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun; cannot stand alone; is a sentence fragment on its own
- **Subordinating conjunctions:** because, although, while, unless, since
- **Relative pronouns:** that, which, who
- Dependent clauses attach to independent clauses to add background information and form complete sentences

## Practice

For each pair below, combine the independent and dependent clause into one complete sentence. Then identify which part is the independent clause and which is the dependent clause.

1. Independent: *The dog barked.* Dependent: *because it heard a noise*
2. Independent: *She finished her homework.* Dependent: *although she was tired*
3. Independent: *We can go to the park.* Dependent: *unless it rains*
4. Independent: *He found the keys.* Dependent: *that he lost last week*

Write your combined sentences on a separate sheet of paper.
